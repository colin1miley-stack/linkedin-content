Single-threaded win rate: 20%.
Multi-threaded: 42%.

The gap isn't your pitch. It's not your pricing. It's not even your product. It's your contact map.

That gap cost me €47k once. I wasn't going to let it happen again.

I used to think multi-threading meant "cc more people." It isn't.

It's building direct relationships with distinct roles across the buying committee.

Here's what I do now, call by call:

Call 1 — Map the committee
"Who else needs to be in the room for this to move?"
"What's the approval process look like on your side?"
"Who could kill this deal, and what would they want to see?"

I write down every name. Then I research each one before the next touch.

Call 2 — Bring in the economic buyer
No proxies. No "I'll ask my manager." I get the VP or CFO on a separate 20-minute call focused on ROI, payback period, and risk.

Between calls — Stakeholder-specific updates
The CFO gets a one-page business case. The champion gets a progress email they can forward. The end user gets a workflow walkthrough.

Same deal. Different story for each person. Because a CFO doesn't care about your UI, and an end user doesn't care about your payback period.

What I see auditing Irish B2B teams

Most teams I work with have 60-70% of deals single-threaded. The rep is proud of their "strong relationship" with the champion. Meanwhile the deal has been in "proposal sent" for six weeks because procurement hasn't even seen the pricing yet.

The rep thinks they're managing one relationship well. They're managing one point of failure.

Your action for this week

Pull your top 5 open deals. Count the active buyer-side contacts in each one.

If any deal has fewer than 3 contacts with clear roles tagged in your CRM, that's your Monday job. Not more demos. Not more follow-up emails. More relationships.

Three contacts minimum. Champion, economic buyer, and one more. Tag them. Engage them directly. Make sure each one knows why this matters to them specifically.

The deals that survive reorgs, budget freezes, and priority shifts are the ones with multiple people inside the account who want them to close.

One champion isn't a deal. It's a foothold. Three is a deal.

Subject: Your pipeline says €4M. You'll close €1.2M. Here's why.

I sat in a forecast call last year where a VP of Sales announced €4.2M in commit for the quarter.

We closed €1.1M.

The VP blamed "macro headwinds." The CFO blamed the VP. The reps blamed the leads. Everyone had someone to blame except the real culprit: the pipeline was fiction from day one.

Nineteen percent. That's the number.

Ebsta and Pavilion's 2025 GTM Benchmarks report analyzed $48 billion in pipeline data across roughly 2,000 revenue teams. The average B2B SaaS win rate fell to 19% in 2025. Down from 29% in 2024.

Nineteen percent. That means 4 in 5 deals currently sitting in your pipeline will not close.

But most teams don't act like they know this. They track total pipeline value. They celebrate "€4M in commit." They miss quarter after quarter and blame the market, the economy, or the product.

The market is fine. Your measurement is broken.

What I see auditing Irish B2B teams

I run Revenue Leak Audits for Irish SaaS companies. Every single one has the same three problems:

Stagnant deals. 40% of open pipeline hasn't had meaningful activity in 30+ days. The rep still lists it as "commit" because the prospect "said they're interested."

Fantasy close dates. Deals with close dates in this quarter that have been pushed forward two or three times. The probability field still shows 70%. Nobody updated it.

Ghost stages. Deals sitting in "proposal sent" for 6, 8, 12 weeks with no next step scheduled. The rep thinks silence means "still considering." Silence means dead.

The forecast accuracy gap

Here's the number that changed how I run pipeline reviews:

Teams with weekly pipeline velocity tracking achieve 87% forecast accuracy.
Teams that track irregularly? 52%.

That 35-point gap is the difference between a board that trusts your number and a board that stops believing anything you say.

The Monday hygiene ritual (20 minutes)

I don't use dashboards with 12 charts. I use a checklist with three questions. Every Monday at 9am, I pull three reports:

1. The stale deal list
Deals with no activity in 14+ days. For each one, the rep answers one question: "What's the next concrete step, and when is it happening?"

If there's no answer, the deal comes out of commit. Period.

2. The close date reality check
Deals with close dates this quarter that have been pushed more than once. These get reviewed individually. Either the date is real and the deal is alive, or the date is wishful thinking and the deal is dead.

3. The probability gut check
Weighted pipeline vs. commit. If my weighted number is more than 30% below my commit, I know someone is sandbagging or someone is delusional. Either way, we talk about it before Wednesday.

Why this works

Pipeline hygiene isn't about having better data. It's about having honest data.

A deal sitting in "proposal sent" for 10 weeks with no next step isn't a deal. It's a wish. And wishes don't close.

When you force your team to confront the stale deals every Monday, two things happen. First, your forecast gets honest. Second, your reps start qualifying harder because they know that ghost deal is going to show up on the stale list every week until they kill it or revive it.

Your action for this week

Block 20 minutes this Monday. Pull those three reports. Be ruthless.

Don't ask "Is this deal still alive?" Ask "When did we last hear from them?" and "What's the next step?"

If the answer to either is vague, the deal is out of commit.

Clean the pipeline, and the number starts telling the truth. Do it this Monday. Don't wait for the quarter-end panic.

---

### COLIN MILEY (Personal Post)

"Proposal sent" is not progress.

It's where deals go to die.

I used to think sending a proposal meant momentum. The buyer asked for it, right? They must be interested.

Then I looked at my numbers. 80% of deals I marked "proposal sent" never came back. Not "lost to competitor." Just... silent. Ghosted.

The proposal graveyard is real. And it's not a pricing problem or a product problem. It's a follow-through problem.

Here's what changed my close rate:

→ Send the proposal with a scheduled follow-up call already on the calendar. Not "let me know if you have questions." A specific time.

→ Within 48 hours, send a one-page summary of the proposal. Not the full doc. The highlights. Make it easy to forward to people who weren't in the room.

→ At day 5, if you haven't heard back, call. Don't email. Call. Ask: "What's the internal process from here?"

The teams that treat "proposal sent" as the start of a new phase — not the end of the old one — close 2x more deals.

Don't send and pray. Send and plan.

### NEWSLETTER / EMAIL VERSION

Subject: 80% of deals die after you send the proposal. Here's the fix.

I used to celebrate "proposal sent" as a milestone.

The buyer asked for pricing. I sent the PDF. I updated the CRM to "proposal sent" and moved on to the next deal, feeling good about the momentum.

Then I actually looked at my data. 80% of deals I marked "proposal sent" in 2024 never came back. I counted.

Not "lost to competitor." Not "budget frozen." Just silent. Ghosted. Sent to the proposal graveyard.

We treat "proposal sent" as progress. It's not. It's a handoff from you to the buyer — and most buyers drop it.

Per Ebsta/Pavilion's 2025 data, the average B2B sales cycle has lengthened 22% since 2022. It's now 84 days from first touch to close. In that stretch, your buyer talks to their CFO, legal, procurement, end users, and three other vendors. Your proposal sits in their inbox while they handle fires that feel more urgent than your deal.

Silence doesn't mean "no." It means "not now, and I forgot about you."

Here's why the silence kills you:

Delayed deals — ones that sit without activity after proposal — reduce win rates by 113% according to Ebsta/Pavilion's 2025 data.

Early decision-maker involvement boosts win rates by 55%.

The pattern is clear: the faster you move after sending a proposal, and the more people you keep engaged during the wait, the more deals you close.

I changed three things. My close rate changed with them.

1. The proposal never goes out alone

When I send a proposal, I also book the follow-up call before the email leaves my outbox. Not "let me know if you have questions." A specific time: "I'll send this over now. Can we block 30 minutes next Tuesday to walk through it? I'll put a hold in your calendar."

If they won't commit to a follow-up, the proposal isn't ready to send.

2. The 48-hour one-pager

Within two business days, I send a one-page summary. Not the full proposal. The highlights: what they're buying, what it costs, what happens next, and what I need from them.

This isn't for the person I sent the proposal to. It's for the people they forward it to — the CFO who wasn't in the demo, the legal person who needs to review terms, the end user who wants to know if this actually helps them.

Most proposals die because the internal champion has to explain your value to their committee from memory. Give them a one-pager they can forward.

3. The day-5 phone call

If I haven't heard back by day 5, I call. I don't email. I don't send a "just checking in" message. I call and ask one question:

"What's the internal process from here?"

Not "did you get my proposal?" Not "do you have any questions?" Those are rep questions. "What's the internal process?" is a buyer question. It assumes the deal is moving and asks what needs to happen next.

Sometimes the answer is "procurement needs to review it." Great — now I know the timeline and I can engage procurement directly.

Sometimes the answer is "we're still evaluating." Fine — "who else are you evaluating, and what would need to be true for us to win?"

Sometimes the answer is awkward silence. That's data too. Better to know at day 5 than at day 35.

Most reps don't do this. Here's why:

Fear. Sending a proposal feels like progress. Following up aggressively feels like being pushy.

It's not pushy. It's professional. You did the work. You built the business case. You owe it to yourself and the buyer to see it through.

The reps who close 2x more proposals aren't better at pitching. They're better at what happens after the pitch.

Do this today:

Look at your open deals in "proposal sent" right now. Count them.

For each one, answer: When is the next scheduled interaction? If the answer is "none" or "I'll follow up next week," fix that today.

Book a call. Send the one-pager. Make the day-5 call.

The proposal graveyard is full of €20k, €50k, €100k deals that died from neglect. Not from a bad pitch. From silence. Don't let yours be one of them.

Talk next Friday,
Colin

P.S. — If your proposal template doesn't include a "next steps" section on page 1, add one. Buyers are busy. Tell them exactly what happens if they say yes. Most proposals bury the process on page 8. By then, they've checked out.
