# Tip #9: Your Discovery Call Is a Qualification Call

**Publish date:** Friday, 3 October 2026
**Channels:** LinkedIn (Colin Miley + Revenue Systems), Website, Kit

---

## LinkedIn Post Version

I used to run discovery calls like pitch practice.

Ask three surface-level questions about "pain points." Then launch into the demo. 40 minutes of features the prospect didn't ask for.

Close rate: 19%. I blamed the leads.

Then I looked at the data. 60% of sales leaders say **poor discovery is the #1 reason for lost deals.** Reps who run thorough discovery have **47% higher win rates** than those who don't.

The problem wasn't the leads. It was that I was using discovery to confirm my pitch, not to disqualify bad deals.

**I changed one thing: the discovery call became a qualification call.**

If I can't answer these four questions by the end, the deal doesn't advance:

→ What exactly is broken and what does it cost them per month?
→ Who else is involved in this decision and what do they each need to see?
→ What happens if they do nothing? (Not what they want. What happens.)
→ What needs to be true for them to sign by [specific date]?

The best reps spend 70% of the call listening. They ask 11–14 questions. They disqualify aggressively.

**Structured discovery closes deals 33% faster.** Only 37% of sales orgs provide a framework. The rest wing it.

Stop pitching in discovery. Start qualifying.

## Newsletter / Email Version

**Subject:** 60% of sales leaders say this is the #1 reason they lose deals

I sat in on a discovery call last month as a silent observer.

The rep asked the prospect three questions:

1. "What does your current process look like?"
2. "What isn't working?"
3. "What would an ideal solution do for you?"

Then he talked for 35 minutes about features. The prospect said "interesting" three times. The rep marked it "qualified" and moved it to "demo scheduled."

It died three weeks later. No budget authority. No timeline. No real pain.

**I used to run discovery calls like this.** Most reps do. We think discovery is about understanding the prospect's world so we can pitch better. It's not.

**Discovery is about deciding whether this deal should be in your pipeline at all.**

**The data is unambiguous**

- 60% of sales leaders identify **poor discovery as the primary reason for lost deals** (CareerTrainer.ai, 2026)
- Reps who conduct thorough discovery calls have **47% higher win rates** than those who don't
- Teams using structured discovery frameworks close deals **33% faster**
- Only **37% of sales organizations** provide their reps with structured discovery frameworks
- Reps who ask **11–14 questions** during discovery have the highest success rates

The other 63%? They wing it. And their pipelines are full of deals that were never real.

**Why most discovery calls fail**

Three failure modes I see in every audit:

**1. The pitch-disguised-as-discovery**

Rep asks 2–3 questions then launches into a demo. The "discovery" was just enough to customize the pitch deck. They never validated budget, authority, timeline, or competitive context. The deal advances because the rep wants it to, not because the prospect qualified.

**2. The checkbox exercise**

Rep runs through BANT like a script. "Do you have budget?" "Is there a timeline?" Surface-level answers get surface-level deals. The rep checks the boxes, moves the deal forward, and is surprised when procurement kills it in week 6.

**3. The friend call**

Rep builds great rapport. The prospect loves the conversation. Nobody asks hard questions. The deal feels warm until the prospect goes silent because they were never actually buying.

All three mistakes share one root cause: **the rep is trying to advance the deal, not qualify it.**

**What I do now — the 4-question qualification**

I don't use a 20-point checklist. I use four questions. If I can't answer all four by the end of the call, the deal doesn't advance. No exceptions.

**Question 1: What exactly is broken and what does it cost them?**

Not "what are your pain points?" — that's therapy, not sales.

I ask: "Walk me through the specific moment this breaks. Who does what, where does it slow down, and what happens because of it?"

Then: "If this stays broken for the next 12 months, what does that cost in lost revenue, extra headcount, or missed targets?"

If they can't quantify the pain, it's not painful enough. If they haven't thought about the cost, they're not serious about fixing it.

**Question 2: Who else is involved and what does each person need to see?**

This is where single-threaded deals die. I don't ask "who's the decision-maker?" — that's naive. In B2B SaaS, the "decision-maker" is a committee.

I ask: "When your team bought the last [category] solution, who was in the room? What did each person care about?"

Then I name roles: "Typically we see CFOs care about payback period, VPs care about team efficiency, and end users care about workflow change. Who on your side maps to each?"

If they can't name 3+ people, either they're not far enough along or they're hiding something. Both are disqualifiers.

**Question 3: What happens if they do nothing?**

This is the implication question. Top performers ask **4x more implication questions** than average reps, driving 20–30% higher win rates and 24% larger deal sizes.

I ask: "You've described the problem. Help me understand — if you change nothing, what does Q4 look like? What does next year look like?"

If the answer is "we'd keep doing what we're doing" — no urgency, no deal.

If the answer is "we'd miss our target by €X" or "I'd have to hire 3 more people" — now we have a timeline and a cost of inaction.

**Question 4: What needs to be true for you to sign by [date]?**

This tests for real process and timeline. Not "when do you want to decide?" — that's wishful thinking.

I ask: "Say we're ready to move in 3 weeks. What needs to happen between now and then? Legal review? Procurement? A pilot? Board approval?"

Then: "Has your company done this before? What's the fastest a deal like this has moved?"

If they can't describe the steps, they don't control the process. If the timeline is "maybe Q1" with no milestones, this isn't a Q4 deal.

**The 70/30 rule**

I aim to talk 30% of the time, max. The prospect talks 70%.

This is uncomfortable. Most reps fill silence with pitch. But the data is clear: reps who actively listen and speak only 43% of the time close more deals. I'm aiming for even less — because every minute I'm talking is a minute I'm not learning something that disqualifies or advances the deal.

**The disqualification mindset**

Here's the counterintuitive part: the best discovery calls end with "this isn't a fit."

When you qualify aggressively, three things happen:
1. Your pipeline gets honest — no more fiction deals
2. Your win rate jumps — you're only working real opportunities
3. Your forecasts get accurate — because you know which deals are real

A rep with 10 qualified deals closes more than a rep with 30 unqualified ones. The 47% win rate lift from thorough discovery isn't from better pitching — it's from better filtering.

**Your action for this week**

Pull your last 5 discovery calls. For each one, answer honestly:

1. Could I quantify the prospect's specific cost of inaction?
2. Did I name 3+ stakeholders and their specific needs?
3. Did the prospect describe their internal approval process?
4. Did I set a specific next step with a date, or did I say "I'll send you some info?"

If any answer is "no" — that's your next call's focus. Not the pitch. The qualification.

The deals that close aren't the ones with the best demos. They're the ones that should have closed from the first call.
